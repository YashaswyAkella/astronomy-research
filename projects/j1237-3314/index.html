What is beside this quasar?

An archival investigation of J1237+3314.

By Yashaswy Akella

The short answer

A known quasar has a faint neighbour in the sky. This project examines existing observations to understand whether the objects are related.

The analysis reports a close match between a compact radio position and the faint neighbour. It also reports an emission feature in a spectrum taken towards that neighbour.

This is an unresolved investigation, not a confirmed discovery. The central measurements have not yet been independently reproduced. No new gravitational lens or confirmed dual active galactic nucleus is claimed.

What was the question?

Two points of light that appear close together can have different explanations:

  • They might be two images of one distant object, created when a foreground mass bends its light.
  • They might be separate objects that are physically close.
  • They might be unrelated objects at different distances.

The project began as a search for gravitationally lensed quasars and developed into a closer examination of this previously catalogued pair.

What is a quasar?

A quasar is a very bright centre of a galaxy. Its light is powered by matter falling towards a supermassive black hole.

It is one kind of active galactic nucleus, often shortened to AGN.

The two objects

A is the bright quasar, SDSS J123704.05+331423.8, at redshift approximately 1.288.

B is the faint optical source about 1.62 arcseconds from A. A precise radio position lies very close to B.

An arcsecond measures a small angle on the sky. Angular proximity does not establish physical association.

The objects and the candidate pair were already known. This project did not discover them.

What the analysis reports

1. Radio emission is associated with B's position

The precise radio position is approximately 0.0047 arcseconds from B's Gaia position, compared with approximately 1.619 arcseconds from A.

This supports associating the compact radio emission with B. It does not establish B's distance or prove that it is a second nucleus physically associated with A.

2. A spectrum taken towards B contains a feature

A spectrum separates light by wavelength. The analysis finds extra light near 8529 Å in the spectrum collected towards B.

The feature is reported in both exposures, before correcting for light from A, and in an additional extraction from the preprocessed detector images.

These are internal checks of the same observations, not independent observational confirmation.

3. The feature could be oxygen emission

One possible identification is [O II], emission from singly ionized oxygen. If correct, the gas has a redshift close to A's.

The identification is not unique. Even if correct, the gas might belong to A's surroundings rather than to B.

What remains uncertain?

  • Whether the feature is correctly identified as [O II].
  • Whether the gas belongs to B or to A's surroundings.
  • Whether A and B are physically associated.
  • Whether this feature or interpretation was previously reported.

Atmospheric blurring sends some light from A into the fibre aimed towards B. Some radio images also blend their positions. The observations were taken at different times and with different instruments.

The project has not definitively ruled out gravitational lensing or confirmed a dual AGN.

What would help next?

Independent examination of the original measurements is the immediate priority. Better-resolved spectroscopy could test where the gas is located. Sharper radio imaging could clarify the radio structure.
